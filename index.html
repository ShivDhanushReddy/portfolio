The Challenge

Scale Model Y production from 5,200 → 7,560 units/week (+44%) at Giga Berlin. Analyze supplier bottlenecks, warehouse capacity, then respond live to a Red Sea shipping disruption that pushed one supplier's lead time from 30 to 44 days — a curveball dropped mid-competition.

Key Findings & Strategy
🔍
4 Critical Single-Source Suppliers

four critical component suppliers — none could physically scale to target volume without intervention.

🎯
Gate-Controlled Milestone Strategy

Stage 1 (6,200/week) treated as a live audit checkpoint before committing resources to Stage 2 — reducing ramp risk significantly.

📦
Warehouse Crisis — No Expansion Needed

Freed capacity through safety stock reallocation using square-root/ramp-factor scaling — pulled from low-risk parts, reinforced high-risk single-source ones.

🚢
Live Red Sea Disruption Response

Updated the strategy with an air-freight bridge solution and honest framing: "safety stock buys time, not resilience."

🥉 3rd Place of all competing teams

"The simplest, most understandable solution — and covered all necessary points."

Jury feedback. 1st place won on pitch theatrics. 2nd built a live AI dashboard. This team won on engineering clarity and rigour.

💡 Lessons from the Floor

The biggest lesson from Gigathon wasn't technical — it was about how ramp-up problems actually fail. You go in expecting a capacity problem. What you find is a dependency problem. The bottleneck isn't the factory floor; it's the four suppliers nobody stress-tested because production was stable. When you push volume by 44%, every single-source component becomes a potential line stop.

The second lesson: safety stock is a buffer, not a strategy. You can buy yourself 14 days with smart reallocation. What you can't do is use that time to do nothing. The Red Sea curveball taught me that the right response to a disruption isn't a new plan — it's an updated one. Keep the structure, change the numbers, be honest about what the data actually says.

That's what I brought from the KIA and WayCool floor: production problems rarely need more complexity. They need clearer thinking about what breaks first and why.
